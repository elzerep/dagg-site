# Dagg — design language

The rules behind the Arrowhead deck, written down so the next thing anyone
builds arrives in the same voice without having to remember why.

Two halves. **How it looks** is the shorter one. **How it speaks** is the one
that actually decides whether the work lands.

---

## 1 · What we are, in the terms the design has to serve

Dagg sits between two things the market usually buys separately: the strategic
judgement to work out where a company's value is actually leaking, and the
engineering capacity to build the thing that fixes it. Most firms have one.

**That combination is never stated. It is proved, page by page.** A page of
judgement, then a page of engineering. An artifact a technical reader can
inspect. A recommendation that arrives with what it would cost to build,
because we would be the ones building it.

Any sentence that claims the combination out loud destroys it. The reader has
to arrive at it themselves; that is what makes it stick.

### The feeling to hit

Exclusive, and inviting. The balance is the whole thing: it has to carry
intelligence and a sense of the future without becoming another hard technology
company, and it has to feel elegant and human without becoming soft.

Confident enough not to boast. Warm enough that a reader leans in rather than
braces. Unusual enough to be remembered, calm enough to be trusted.

If a page reads as a technology corporation, it is wrong. If it reads as a
consultancy, it is wrong. It should read as neither, which is what Dagg is.

## 2 · How it speaks

### The register

**Confident understatement.** Ambition comes from the specificity and scale of
what is described — never from adjectives about Dagg. This is a Swedish
register: superlatives read as insecurity to a senior audience.

Three rules, in order of how often they are broken:

**State, don't qualify.** If something is true, say it in the shortest sentence
available and move on. Do not add a softening clause to prove you are humble.

**No adjectives about Dagg. Ever.** Not leading, world-class, elite, unique,
best-in-class, cutting-edge, proven, trusted, passionate. If a sentence
contains praise of Dagg, delete the praise — what remains is usually the
sentence you wanted.

**No performed humility either.** "We would rather be tested than believed" is
not modesty, it is a boast wearing modesty's coat. Replace every such sentence
with an actual test.

### The hedging budget

Disclaimers are not integrity. Twenty-nine negations across a document reads as
a firm that has rehearsed being accused.

The test for every qualifying sentence: **if this said nothing at all, would a
reasonable reader form a false belief?** If no, the disclaimer is decoration —
delete it. If yes, it is load-bearing — keep it and say it with confidence,
because a provenance statement is not a hedge.

At most **two** qualifications in a document's footers. Not eight.

### Never said

| Never | Because |
|---|---|
| "consultant", "consultancy" | We are a transformation partner. The word sells hours. |
| Any competitor, or any category of firm | Defining yourself against others is the posture of the weaker party — and the firm you are dismissing may be sitting in the client's building. |
| ARR, licence revenue, how the business model earns | An exclusive company does not publish its revenue engine. That belongs in a conversation. |
| Any invented number | A figure with no measurement behind it is decoration, and a technical reader will ask where it came from. |
| Any productivity multiple | Not 5x, not 10x, not "materially faster". |
| "Recommended", "Most popular" as a badge | A badge is a category label that applies to everyone. A judgement addressed to the reader is not. |
| Any individual's name, tenure, or output compared to anyone's | Holds even if the other side raises it first. |

### Said carefully

The business model **at altitude only**: we transform, the technology we build
keeps working afterwards, and some transformations become companies of their
own. Never the mechanics.

**Aloi is the proof, not a case study.** It demonstrates that a workflow problem
can become its own platform. It belongs early and with room, not in a footnote.

### Headlines

Conclusion-led, never topic labels. "Where the time actually goes" beats
"Our approach". A headline that states a finding earns the page; a headline
that names a category asks the reader to do the work.

## 3 · How it looks

### Document scale, not poster scale

The single most common failure. A headline at billboard size with three lines
under it is a poster: loud, empty, and it reads as having nothing to say.
Exclusivity comes from restraint — smaller type, more air *between* elements
and less at the edges, a grid you can feel, hairlines rather than borders.

Target ratio of headline to caption is roughly **6:1 on a single-idea page**,
not 3:1 and not 12:1.

### Never empty, never crowded

Every page carries **three registers**: a header, a main argument, and a band of
supporting fact along the bottom. The band is what stops a page reading as
under-filled without resorting to decoration — it adds substance, not air.

Air is composed, not left over. If content occupies the top third and the rest
is blank, that is not negative space, it is an unfinished page.

### The spine

A full-height rail down the left of every page: page number, section label set
vertically, a hairline. It is what makes a set of pages read as a document
rather than as slides, and it fills the margin with structure instead of
nothing.

### Two grounds only

Light carries the argument. Night marks a beat — an opening, a pivot, a close.
Three or four dark pages in fifteen, never two adjacent. A third ground with a
3% luminance step is invisible when projected and is not restraint, it is a
wasted variable.

### One accent

Used as a hairline, a mark, a single field — not as a surface. Spend boldness
in one place per page and keep everything around it quiet. The second colour
(green) appears at most once in a document.

### Type

**A deck and a website are different reading situations, and the type has to
change between them.** A deck is glanced at while someone talks; a website is
read alone, for minutes. Porting deck typography to a site unchanged produces
something that looks right in a screenshot and is tiring to read.

Two rules came out of getting this wrong:

**Use a text serif, not a display serif.** A display serif has high stroke
contrast and thin hairlines — designed to be looked at in one glance. In running
text those same letterforms read as sharp and effortful.

**Headlines are grotesk. The serif is reserved for the phrase inside a headline
that should sound spoken** — set in italic, two or three words, never the whole
line. Setting every headline in a serif reads as formal and traditional, which
is right for a printed proposal and stiff on a screen.

Three roles, no more. A grotesk for structure and body. **A serif for a spoken
voice** — a question, a line someone would say aloud — and never for dense text;
that assignment is what gives the serif meaning. A mono for metadata, page
numbers and diagram labels, small and widely tracked.

Body weight is **400 on light grounds, never 300.** Thin light type on a light
ground is what tires the eye, and it is the most common reason a page that
looks elegant in a screenshot is unreadable in a room.

### Diagrams

A document that claims to map how work happens must show a map. A diagram earns
its place when it depicts the mechanism rather than illustrating the topic.

**Label the edges with metric names, not invented values.** Showing *which*
edges get measured is the insight; fabricating numbers for them is the failure.

Every illustrative diagram carries its provenance in plain words.

### Count the devices

Clutter is a count, not a size. A page carrying a vertical rail label, a mono
eyebrow, mono metadata keys, mono row numbers, mono diagram labels, hairlines
between every zone and italic emphasis is running seven signals at once, and it
reads as busy however carefully each one is set.

**The palette and the type have to agree.** A hard dark ground supports hard
typography — tight tracking, dense uppercase metadata, italic emphasis. Move
the ground to warm and light and those same devices fight it. When the colour
softens, the type has to soften with it, and the way to soften type is mostly
to remove things.

Mono survives in two places: the section eyebrow, and diagram labels. Everywhere
else metadata is sentence-case sans at reading size.

**No italic for emphasis.** It reads as instructing the reader which words
matter. Use weight, or write the sentence so the emphasis is in the words.

### No card grids

Structure comes from hairlines, columns and whitespace. A grid of bordered
cards is the default that every template produces, and it reads as one.

## 4 · The mechanics

Tokens live in `tokens.css`. Nothing defines a colour outside it.

**Two accent variants** exist while the identity is being decided:
`data-accent="violet"` (Dagg's own, default) and `data-accent="coral"`.

Measured against the light ground `#FAF9F5`:

| | contrast | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| violet `#5B3BC4` | 6.95:1 | text and graphic |
| green `#2F6141` | 6.86:1 | text and graphic |
| coral `#D97757` | **2.96:1** | **graphic only — never text** |

That is a constraint, not a preference. Coral is a beautiful field colour and
an unusable text colour on ivory. The variant exists so the choice can be made
by looking rather than by argument.

Every text-on-background pair ships at 4.5:1 or better. Captions carry content
and are held to the same bar as body — they are not decoration.

## 5 · Before anything ships

- [ ] Read every headline aloud. Any sentence that praises Dagg, or softens a true statement to prove humility, is rewritten or deleted.
- [ ] Count the qualifications in the footers. More than two, cut.
- [ ] No invented number anywhere, including in an illustration.
- [ ] The hybrid is unmistakable and never stated.
- [ ] The word "consultant" does not appear.
- [ ] No competitor and no category of firm is named or characterised.
- [ ] Every text-on-background pair measured, not assumed.
- [ ] No page has content in the top third and nothing below it.
- [ ] Render it and look at every page. Not a sample — every page.
